## Summary

The Ebola virus disease (EVD) outbreak that began in the Meliandou village of Guinea's Guéckédou Prefecture in December 2013 — tracing to a two-year-old boy who died on December 6, 2013 — escalated dramatically in 2014, spreading to Liberia and Sierra Leone by March. The World Health Organization (WHO) declared the outbreak a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) on August 8, 2014, by which point the outbreak had claimed nearly 1,000 lives — approximately 961 deaths among some 1,779 confirmed, probable, and suspected cases — with the actual toll estimated substantially higher due to underreporting.

The Ebola virus (species *Zaire ebolavirus*) carries a fatality rate of 25–90% depending on the viral strain and quality of supportive care available. In the West African context, where health infrastructure was severely underdeveloped, case fatality rates in certain areas exceeded 70%. The virus spreads through direct contact with the bodily fluids of symptomatic individuals or deceased persons, not through airborne transmission; this mechanism made community and burial practices a critical transmission vector.

The outbreak differed structurally from all 24 previous Ebola epidemics recorded since the disease's identification in 1976: it occurred primarily in urban centres — Conakry (population ~2 million), Freetown (~1 million), and Monrovia (~1 million) — rather than isolated rural villages. Urban transmission dynamics, combined with porous borders between Guinea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone and limited cross-border health surveillance, enabled exponential spread that outpaced containment efforts for months.

## Timeline of the Outbreak

**December 2013**: Index case identified in Guéckédou, Guinea. Local health authorities initially suspect cholera.

**March 2014**: Guinea's Ministry of Health confirms Ebola; cases reported in Liberia. Médecins Sans Frontières deploys to Guinea and issues warnings that the outbreak is "unprecedented" in its geographic spread.

**June 2014**: WHO and CDC raise alarm as case counts continue rising and cross-border transmission is confirmed. MSF warns that the global response is "dangerously inadequate."

**August 8, 2014**: WHO Director-General Margaret Chan declares a PHEIC — the highest level of international public health alert — after an emergency committee meeting. Confirmed and probable case count stands at approximately 1,779, with 961 deaths. At this point the epidemic is doubling every 15–20 days in the worst-affected areas.

**August 2014**: First American cases evacuated: Dr. Kent Brantly and medical worker Nancy Writebol are airlifted to Emory University Hospital in Atlanta for treatment, receiving the experimental drug ZMapp — the first use of the drug in humans. Their recoveries draw global attention to the potential of experimental therapeutics.

**September 2014**: The United States announces deployment of 3,000 military personnel to West Africa to assist with logistics, laboratory capacity, and treatment unit construction. The UN Security Council meets in an emergency session and declares Ebola a "threat to international peace and security."

**October 2014**: Cases appear in the United States (Thomas Eric Duncan, who traveled from Liberia, dies in Dallas on October 8); two of his nurses become infected, briefly intensifying domestic American fear. Spain records a transmission within Europe involving a nurse who had cared for repatriated missionaries.

**Late 2014 – early 2015**: Case counts in Liberia begin declining following intensive community mobilisation and international response scaling; Guinea and Sierra Leone remain high. The first experimental Ebola vaccine trials begin.

**January 2016**: WHO declares the West African Ebola epidemic over. Final toll: 28,616 confirmed, probable, and suspected cases; 11,310 deaths across ten countries.

## The Scale of Disruption

Healthcare systems in all three primarily affected countries suffered cascading failure. By August 2014, Médecins Sans Frontières reported that treatment centres were full and turning away patients. An estimated 500 healthcare workers died during the epidemic — a catastrophic loss in countries where one doctor serves tens of thousands of patients. Liberia, which had roughly 50 physicians for a population of 4.5 million at the outbreak's start, lost a significant portion of this workforce.

Economic disruption was severe and immediate. The World Bank estimated a US$1.6 billion GDP loss for Guinea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone in 2014–2015, driven by:
- Collapse of agricultural production as rural populations fled, quarantines prevented harvest, and supply chains failed
- Near-cessation of cross-border trade and informal market activity
- Withdrawal of foreign investment and suspension of mining operations
- Airline service suspensions by multiple carriers including British Airways and Air France

Schools in Liberia and Sierra Leone were closed for months; an estimated 5 million children lost access to education for portions of 2014–2015. Food insecurity spiked as staple food prices rose 30–80% in urban markets.

Survivors faced lasting health complications (post-Ebola syndrome), including joint pain, vision impairment, and neurological effects, as well as severe social stigmatisation that impeded reintegration into communities.

## International Response and Policy Legacy

The initial response was widely criticised as inadequate and slow. MSF repeatedly issued public statements urging greater international mobilisation months before the PHEIC declaration. The UN Mission for Ebola Emergency Response (UNMEER) — the first UN emergency health mission ever established — was created on September 19, 2014. At peak deployment in December 2014, international responders included personnel from the United States, United Kingdom, France, China, Cuba, and more than 50 other nations.

The epidemic catalysed lasting changes in global health architecture:
- The WHO underwent reform processes to improve its emergency response capacity, leading to creation of the Health Emergencies Programme in 2016
- The Global Health Security Agenda, launched in February 2014 just as the outbreak began, gained renewed funding and political commitment
- The Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations (CEPI) was founded at Davos in January 2017 with US$460 million in initial funding, specifically to accelerate vaccine development against epidemic-prone pathogens
- The rVSV-ZEBOV vaccine (trade name Ervebo) was developed and deployed in ring vaccination trials during the subsequent 2018–2020 Democratic Republic of Congo outbreak, drawing directly on lessons from 2014–2016

The West African Ebola epidemic became a foundational case study for pandemic preparedness modelling, informing responses to subsequent outbreaks including COVID-19 in 2020.
